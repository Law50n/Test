# Research brief: The Dyatlov Pass Incident (1959)

Case check: yes, this comfortably supports a real 15-25+ minute episode.
There's a full timeline, named victims with individually documented
injuries, a real 2019-2021 scientific reopening with a named peer-reviewed
study, and at least four genuinely distinct theories with real proponents
-- not one thin fact stretched out.

## Established facts

- On 23/01/1959, a group of ten experienced hikers/skiers, mostly students
  and graduates of the Ural Polytechnic Institute, set out on a
  winter trek in the Northern Urals led by 23-year-old Igor Dyatlov.
  [Wikipedia: Dyatlov Pass incident; History.com]
- One member, Yuri Yudin, turned back early due to illness and survived;
  the remaining nine continued. [Wikipedia; Mental Floss]
- The nine: Igor Dyatlov (23), Zinaida Kolmogorova (22), Rustem Slobodin
  (23), Nikolai Thibeaux-Brignolles (23), Lyudmila Dubinina (20),
  Alexander Kolevatov (24), Yuri Doroshenko, Yuri Krivonishchenko, and
  Semyon Zolotaryov (37). [Mental Floss; dyatlovpass.com]
- Zolotaryov was a last-minute addition -- a World War II veteran and
  professional ski/mountain instructor who joined to help the group earn
  a hiking certification, reportedly telling them he wanted to see his
  mother before the season got busy. He introduced himself to the group
  as "Sasha"/"Alexander" rather than his real first name, for reasons
  that were never established. [dyatlovpass.com: Semyon Zolotaryov]
- The group planned to send a telegram on their expected return around
  12/02/1959; when nothing came by 20/02, a search was organized.
  [Wikipedia; History.com]
- On 26/02/1959, searchers found the group's tent on the slope of Kholat
  Syakhl ("Dead Mountain" in the Mansi language) -- cut open from the
  inside, and abandoned with the hikers' boots, warm clothing, and
  belongings still inside. [Wikipedia; History.com; Mental Floss]
- Footprints (some bare, some in socks or a single boot) led away from
  the tent toward a treeline roughly 1.5 km away, in temperatures
  estimated around -25 to -30 C. [Wikipedia; History.com]
- The first two bodies (Krivonishchenko, Doroshenko) were found under a
  large cedar tree at the treeline, partially undressed, with broken
  branches on the tree suggesting someone had climbed it, apparently to
  see something over the tent from a distance. [Wikipedia; Mental Floss]
- Three more (Dyatlov, Kolmogorova, Slobodin) were found between the
  cedar and the tent, in postures suggesting they died trying to walk
  back. [Wikipedia]
- The remaining four (Dubinina, Zolotaryov, Kolevatov, Thibeaux-Brignolles)
  were not found until May 1959, buried under several meters of snow in a
  ravine further from the tent. [Wikipedia; History.com]
- Autopsy findings: six died of hypothermia; the four found in the ravine
  had severe injuries with no matching external wounds -- Thibeaux-
  Brignolles had a fatal skull fracture, and Zolotaryov and Dubinina both
  had multiple broken ribs causing fatal internal bleeding (Dubinina:
  four broken ribs on one side, seven on the other). Dubinina was also
  missing her eyes, tongue, and some facial soft tissue; Zolotaryov's
  eye sockets were also empty. Investigators attributed this to
  decomposition/scavenging in the ravine rather than an attack.
  [ermakvagus.com autopsy records; Wikipedia]
- The original 1959 regional prosecutor's report explicitly noted the
  "absence of external bodily injuries and signs of struggle on all
  corpses," all valuables were untouched, and there was no evidence of
  any footprints at the scene other than the nine hikers' own -- ruling
  out an attack by other people. [Wikipedia; dyatlovpass.com]
- Some clothing items (including Krivonishchenko's) tested positive for
  elevated radioactive trace contamination; the source was never
  established. [Wikipedia; History.com]
- The Mansi, an Indigenous people living in the region, were the
  original investigation's prime suspects, but were cleared: no evidence
  of any outside presence at the scene, no history of hostility, and the
  location wasn't used by them for hunting or herding in winter.
  [dyatlovpass.com: Mansi trail; Wikipedia]
- A separate group of hikers about 50 km south reported seeing strange
  glowing orange spheres in the night sky on 01/02/1959; similar sightings
  were independently reported by others (including Soviet meteorological
  and military observers) over the following weeks. Their connection to
  the Dyatlov group's deaths, if any, was never established.
  [Wikipedia; multiple news summaries]
- The original Soviet investigation closed in May 1959, attributing the
  deaths to an unnamed "compelling natural force," and the file was
  classified for decades. [Wikipedia; History.com]
- In February 2019, Russian authorities (the Prosecutor General's office,
  under Andrei Kuryakov) reopened the case using modern forensic methods.
  In July 2020, they concluded an avalanche was the most likely trigger.
  [TASS: "Prosecutors say avalanche killed Dyatlov group..."; dyatlovpass.com]
- In January 2021, engineers Alexander Puzrin (ETH Zurich) and Johan
  Gaume (EPFL) published a peer-reviewed physical model in Nature's
  Communications Earth & Environment showing a small slab avalanche was
  physically plausible on the slope's actual (shallower-than-typical)
  incline, given how the hikers had cut into the snow to pitch their
  tent and given local katabatic wind loading -- and why it could leave
  little visible trace by the time searchers arrived weeks later.
  [Puzrin & Gaume, Communications Earth & Environment, Jan 2021; EPFL press release]

## Theories, attributed

- **Slab avalanche (Soviet/Russian official conclusion, 2020; supported
  by Puzrin & Gaume's 2021 physical model)**: a delayed small slab of
  wind-loaded snow slid onto the tent, injuring some occupants and
  panicking the rest into cutting their way out and fleeing without
  proper clothing in the dark, then dying of hypothermia and (for four
  of them) a fall into the ravine in the darkness/whiteout.
  *For*: matches the tent damage, the flight without boots, and now has
  a peer-reviewed physical mechanism explaining a small slide on a
  gentle slope. *Against*: the original 1959 investigators who visited
  the actual site found no visual avalanche debris, and some
  Dyatlov-case researchers argue a slide small enough to leave no trace
  seems too small to inflict Dubinina's and Zolotaryov's specific
  injuries.
- **Infrasound-induced panic (popularized by American author Donnie
  Eichar, "Dead Mountain," 2013)**: wind passing over the dome-shaped
  peak created a Karman vortex street, generating infrasound below human
  hearing that can induce nausea, disorientation, and panic, driving the
  group out of the tent in blind fear. *For*: would explain fleeing
  without dressing, in a way independent of any physical avalanche
  evidence. *Against*: is difficult to test or confirm after the fact,
  and doesn't obviously explain the ravine group's specific traumatic
  injuries.
- **Katabatic wind (independent of Eichar's infrasound framing; discussed
  by Soviet meteorologists and later researchers)**: extreme, sudden
  downslope winds specific to the local terrain, severe enough to make
  staying in or near the tent seem untenable. *For*: matches the region's
  documented wind patterns. *Against*: doesn't fully explain the crush
  injuries either.
- **Military weapons testing (parachute mines/ordnance)** -- raised
  informally by some later investigators and writers given confirmed
  Soviet military activity and missile testing in the wider region
  around that time. *For*: parachute-type mines can cause severe internal
  injury with little external wound, matching the ravine group.
  *Against*: the original and 2019-2020 re-investigations found no
  explosive residue, crater, or other physical evidence at the site, and
  no record of a test in that specific location on that date.
- **Attack by the Mansi or other people** -- the original 1959
  investigators' first working theory. *Status*: formally ruled out by
  that same investigation (see facts above) -- included here only
  because it's a real, documented step in the case's history, not
  because the evidence supports it.

No credible investigator or serious researcher has proposed a named
living individual as responsible; nothing in this case implicates any
identifiable private person, so the "no naming a living private
individual" rule isn't a live concern for this script, but the
theories above still need the same hedged, attributed language in the
draft (see docs/episode-workflow.md).

# Research brief: The Mary Celeste (1872)

Case check: yes. A complete, well-documented timeline, ten real named
people who vanished without a trace, zero graphic content in the actual
record (no blood, no bodies, no violence ever found), and a genuinely
useful "popular myth vs. real record" angle, since much of what people
think they know about this case is a 19th-century short story, not
history. Excellent fit for the calm tone -- if anything, the real,
sparse facts are eerier than the embellished version.

## Established facts

- The brigantine Mary Celeste left New York on 07/11/1872, bound for
  Genoa, Italy, carrying roughly 1,700 barrels of denatured (industrial)
  alcohol. [Britannica; History.com]
- Aboard: Captain Benjamin Briggs, an experienced, well-regarded
  captain who owned a financial share in the ship and cargo; his wife
  Sarah; their two-year-old daughter, Sophia; and a crew of seven --
  first mate Albert Richardson, second mate Andrew Gilling, steward
  Edward Head, and four German seamen (Volkert Lorenzen, Arian Martens,
  Boz Lorenzen, and Gottlieb Goodschaad). Ten people in total. [Britannica;
  various case summaries]
- The night before departure, Briggs dined in New York with Captain
  David Morehouse of another brigantine, the Dei Gratia -- the two men
  were friends, and Morehouse's own ship was due to follow a similar
  route about a week later. [Wikipedia: The Mystery of the Mary
  Celeste; multiple case summaries]
- The ship's last log entry is dated 25/11/1872, recording a position
  near the Azores. [History.com]
- On 05/12/1872, Morehouse's Dei Gratia came across the Mary Celeste
  adrift roughly 400 nautical miles east of the Azores -- sailing,
  under some canvas, with nobody at the helm. Morehouse recognized the
  ship immediately as Briggs's. [Britannica; Smithsonian Magazine]
- A boarding party found the ship deserted but seaworthy: about 3.5
  feet of water in the hold (within a working pump's capacity for a
  wooden ship of that era), one of the ship's two pumps dismantled, and
  a makeshift sounding rod -- used to measure water in the hold -- left
  on deck. [Royal Museums Greenwich; Smithsonian Magazine]
- The ship's single lifeboat/yawl was missing, along with the
  navigation instruments (chronometer and sextant) and the ship's
  papers. The captain's official log book, however, was left behind.
  [Wikipedia; Britannica]
- The cargo was almost entirely intact -- nine of the roughly 1,700
  alcohol barrels were later found empty on arrival in Genoa, the rest
  full. Personal belongings, money, and valuables belonging to the crew
  and the Briggs family were still aboard, undisturbed. [Smithsonian
  Magazine; History.com]
- No sign of violence, struggle, fire, or damage sufficient to sink the
  ship was ever found anywhere aboard. [Britannica; Royal Museums
  Greenwich]
- None of the ten people aboard -- Briggs, his family, or any of the
  seven crew -- were ever seen again, and no bodies were ever recovered.
  [Britannica]
- The Gibraltar vice-admiralty court's inquiry, led by Attorney General
  Frederick Solly-Flood, strongly suspected mutiny, foul play, or
  insurance fraud, and delayed release of the ship and cargo for months
  -- but the inquiry never proved any crime had taken place. [Wikipedia;
  Smithsonian Magazine]
- **What people generally "remember" about this case is largely
  fiction.** In 1884, a young Arthur Conan Doyle published an
  anonymous short story, "J. Habakuk Jephson's Statement," loosely
  based on the case, which invented details -- the ship found in an
  almost pristine, undisturbed state, its lifeboat still aboard -- that
  directly contradict the real record (the yawl was missing, the ship
  had visibly been through rough weather). Later retellings added
  further invented flourishes, like a hot meal still steaming on the
  table. Frederick Solly-Flood, who had led the real Gibraltar inquiry,
  publicly denounced Doyle's story as "a fabrication from beginning to
  end" -- but the embellishments outlived the correction and are still
  widely repeated as fact today. [History Today; LRB; conandoyleinfo.com]
- The ship continued sailing under new owners for another 12 years,
  gaining a reputation as an unlucky vessel. In 1885, her last owner,
  Gilman C. Parker, deliberately grounded her near Port-au-Prince,
  Haiti, loaded with a falsely overvalued cargo, as an insurance fraud
  scheme. The fraud was uncovered, but Parker was never convicted (his
  trial ended without a conviction), and he died in poverty within
  months. [Wikipedia; multiple case summaries] -- this is a separate,
  later incident from the 1872 disappearance and shouldn't be blurred
  into "what happened to the crew."
- In a 2005 BBC-backed reconstruction, chemist Dr. Andrea Sella
  (University College London) demonstrated that a methanol/butane
  vapor explosion, of the kind that could arise from leaking industrial
  alcohol barrels, produces a fast, violent pressure flash capable of
  blowing open hatches and terrifying anyone aboard -- without
  necessarily leaving scorch marks or lasting damage behind. [zmescience;
  BBC coverage] Sella himself called it "the most compelling
  explanation" that "fits the facts best."

## Theories, attributed

- **Alcohol vapor flash/explosion panic (modern leading theory,
  physically demonstrated by chemist Andrea Sella, 2005)**: leaking
  fumes from the alcohol cargo built up and ignited or flashed,
  terrifying Briggs into believing the ship was about to explode, and
  he ordered everyone into the yawl to wait at a safe distance,
  possibly still tethered to the ship. *For*: explains abandoning a
  seaworthy, largely undamaged ship without any sign of violence, and
  matches the found-empty barrels as the fume source. *Against*:
  doesn't fully explain why, if they were simply waiting nearby, nobody
  ever made it back to the ship or was ever found -- usually paired
  with a assumption that the tow line parted or rough weather separated
  the yawl from the ship afterward.
- **Sudden storm, rogue wave, or waterspout (raised by some historians,
  and reportedly favored by a Briggs family descendant)**: violent
  weather made the ship seem like it was sinking or badly damaged,
  prompting a panicked abandonment. *For*: the log's final days
  reportedly note rough conditions. *Against*: no specific weather
  event at that exact time and place was ever verified; it's inferred
  rather than documented.
- **Mutiny or crew violence (the dominant suspicion of Gibraltar's
  Attorney General Frederick Solly-Flood at the time)**: drunken crew
  members turned on Briggs and his family. *For*: was the official
  working theory pursued hardest in 1872-73. *Against*: no blood, no
  sign of struggle, and no weapon damage was ever found aboard, and the
  inquiry that pursued this theory hardest never proved it.
- **Deliberate fraud by Briggs himself (a fringe theory, little
  supported)**: included only because it's part of the case's
  discussed history. *Against*: Briggs held a financial stake in the
  ship and cargo's safe delivery and had no documented motive to
  endanger his own wife and daughter -- most serious historians give
  this theory little weight.

Nobody involved in the 1872 disappearance is alive today, and no living
person is implicated in anything here -- not a live concern for this
script.
